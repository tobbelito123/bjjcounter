Common White Belt Mistakes (and How to Fix Them)

Every white belt makes the same handful of mistakes in their first year. None of it means you're bad at this, it just means you're new. Here's what to watch for.

If terms like guard, mount, or side control still make you nod along without fully understanding them, the BJJ glossary is worth a read before the rest of this.

Muscling Through Everything

New students default to strength because it's the only tool they've got. You see an opening, so you crank an arm or squeeze a guard with your whole body instead of finding the angle that makes the technique easy. It works for a while against other beginners, then stops working the moment you roll with someone more experienced. The fix isn't getting stronger, it's noticing when you're straining. If a technique needs your grip to shake and your face to turn red, you're missing a detail somewhere. Slow down, check your angle, and ask your coach to show you the version that doesn't need muscle at all.

Gripping Like It's the Last Handhold on a Cliff

Watch a white belt roll and you'll usually see both hands locked onto the gi from the first second, squeezing the entire round. It burns your forearms out in under two minutes and gives your opponent nothing to react to, since you've already told them exactly where your hands are. Grips should turn on and off. Take one when it sets up something specific, let it go once it's done its job. Your forearms last longer, and you actually feel what your opponent is doing instead of white-knuckling through the whole exchange.

Forgetting to Breathe

It sounds too basic to matter, but a lot of new students hold their breath under pressure, especially pinned in side control or mount. Holding your breath tightens everything else too: your shoulders, your grip, your patience. It also means you gas out roughly twice as fast as the person breathing normally on top of you. Getting crushed is part of the deal early on. Exhaling through it, on purpose, keeps you calmer and buys you more rounds before you're too tired to think clearly.

Hunting Submissions Instead of Position

Everyone wants the tap. It's the most satisfying part of training and the easiest thing to measure. But chasing a submission from a bad spot, like reaching for an armbar from your back instead of improving your guard first, usually costs you the position and the submission both. Position before submission is a cliché for a reason. Secure the spot, then look for the finish. You'll land more submissions this way, not fewer, because they come from positions you actually control instead of scrambles you're already losing.

Comparing Week Three to Someone Else's Year Three

It's hard not to watch the blue belt smashing everyone and wonder why you can't do that yet. The honest answer is time. There's no shortcut around the hundreds of hours it takes to build the instincts you're watching. Comparing your early rolls to someone years ahead of you is comparing two different sports. A more useful comparison is your own rolls from a month ago, which is a lot easier to do when you've actually written down what happened instead of trying to remember it later.
