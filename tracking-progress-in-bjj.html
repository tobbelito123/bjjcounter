Why Tracking Your BJJ Progress Actually Works

Most of what makes you better at this sport happens too slowly to notice. Writing it down is the only way to actually see it.

Your Memory Isn't Built for This

Ask most white belts how their training is going and you'll get a shrug, or a story about the blue belt who folded them in half last Tuesday. That's not because nothing good happened that week. It's because a rough exchange sticks in your head a lot harder than the quiet win of finally stopping a pass you used to fall for every time. Left to memory alone, you end up grading months of training on whatever happened most recently and most dramatically, which is a terrible way to judge anything.

What's Actually Worth Writing Down

You don't need a spreadsheet with twelve columns. The two things that matter most are what you finished and who you finished it on. A triangle against a fellow white belt and a triangle against a purple belt are not the same data point, and lumping them together hides the fact that you're actually getting sharper against tougher resistance. Keep it that simple and you'll actually keep doing it, which matters more than any amount of detail you'd give up on after a week.

The Pull of a Streak

There's a reason habit apps lean so hard on streaks, and it works the same way on the mat. A running count of consecutive training days or weeks turns showing up into something you don't want to break, on the nights your bed sounds a lot more appealing than driving to the gym. It's a small trick, but it's an honest one. Nobody's grading you on it but you.

A Log Isn't a Substitute for Coaching

None of this replaces what a coach watching your position and posture can tell you. A tally of submissions won't catch a bad habit in your base or a grip you're relying on too much. What it's good for is the thing coaching sessions can't cover, which is the long view of your own trajectory. That's really the same idea behind the recent Jonah Hill "annihilate" story, where the real point buried under the viral clip was that a countable small win, even one nobody's filming, is worth more over time than trying to remember how far you've come.
