Open Guard vs Closed Guard: The Basics

Your instructor says "pass my guard" and you nod like you know what that means. Here's the actual split between the two guard families, so the next class makes more sense.

Closed Guard Is the Starting Point

Closed guard is what most people picture first: you're on your back, your opponent is kneeling or standing between your legs, and your ankles are locked behind their back. That lock is the whole point. It keeps your opponent close, stops them from standing up and walking away, and buys you time to work. Most schools teach it early because it forces you to learn the core idea of guard, which is controlling someone with your legs instead of your arms, before adding any extra moving parts. From closed guard you can set up a long list of classic attacks: armbars, triangles, kimuras, guillotines, and sweeps that flip you on top. Its weakness is just as simple as its strength. Once your opponent learns to break the lock open, which every blue belt eventually does, you're out of it and need a plan for what comes next.

Open Guard Isn't One Position

This is where people get tripped up. "Open guard" doesn't name a single technique the way closed guard does. It's an umbrella term for every guard where your ankles aren't locked around your opponent's back. Butterfly guard, De La Riva, spider guard, X-guard, lasso, reverse De La Riva: all of it is open guard. The common thread across all of them is that you're using your feet and shins to control your opponent's hips, legs, or arms from a distance instead of locking them in close. That gives you more ways to sweep and more angles to attack from, but it also demands more from you. Nothing is locked, so your feet and hands are working constantly just to keep the position from collapsing the moment your opponent shifts their weight.

Why the Split Is Worth Knowing

A lot of curriculums are built around this grouping without saying so out loud. When a coach says a sweep "works from any open guard," they mean the underlying mechanic transfers across butterfly, De La Riva, and the rest, even though the leg positions look different. Knowing that open guard is a category, not a single guard, stops you from wondering why last week's spider guard sweep and this week's X-guard sweep feel so similar. The pace differs too. Closed guard tends to be slower and more deliberate since the lock is doing some of the work for you. Open guard rewards constant small adjustments and tends to move faster, which is also why it shows up so much in no-gi, where there's no fabric to grip and hold anything still.

Which One Should You Learn First

There's no single rule here, and gyms genuinely differ on it. Plenty of schools start white belts in closed guard because the lock gives you a stable place to learn hip movement and posture control before anything else is moving. Other gyms lean into open guard concepts earlier, especially if they teach a lot of no-gi, since that's what students will actually be living in once the gi comes off. Neither approach is wrong. If your gym's order looks different from a video you watched online, that's not a sign anyone is doing it badly, just a different path to the same place.
